## Soak Trail Publishes First Data-Driven Report on Hot Springs in the American West: 686 Springs Mapped Across 8 States

**Idaho has 14x the hot springs density of any other state. 92% of all mapped springs are free to visit.**

---

**Pacific Northwest — January 15, 2026** — Soak Trail, an independent research project documenting natural hot springs across the western United States, today published the *State of Hot Springs in the American West: 2026 Data Report* — the first comprehensive, data-driven analysis of thermal springs in the region.

The report analyzes 686 natural hot springs across 8 states (Alaska, California, Colorado, Idaho, Montana, Oregon, Washington, Wyoming), drawing from USGS water quality data, OpenStreetMap, NOAA thermal spring records, and Recreation.gov campground data.

### Key Findings

- **Idaho dominates.** With 420 mapped springs, Idaho has 61% of all mapped hot springs in the western US — a density of 50.3 springs per 10,000 square miles, 14 times higher than any other state.

- **Nearly all hot springs are free.** 92% of mapped springs (633 of 686) have no recorded fee. Most natural hot springs on National Forest and BLM land are open to the public at no cost.

- **Most hot springs require a hike.** 69% of springs (476 of 686) are accessible only by trail. Only 16% are drive-up and 9% are developed resorts.

- **The ideal soaking range is common.** Of 651 springs with temperature data, 72% (465) fall between 95–110°F — the range most people find comfortable for soaking.

- **Oregon is the most consistent.** All 68 Oregon springs are open year-round, making it the most reliable state for hot springs access regardless of season.

- **Colorado has the highest springs.** The top 10 highest-elevation hot springs in the database are all in Colorado, led by Conundrum Hot Springs at 11,200 feet.

### About the Data

The report is based on Soak Trail's database of 686 natural hot springs, built from public data sources including:

- **USGS National Water Information System** — temperature, pH, conductivity, and mineral content
- **OpenStreetMap** — spring locations, access roads, trails, and nearby amenities
- **NOAA Thermal Springs Dataset** — national thermal spring records
- **Recreation.gov** — campground and recreation area data
- **Wikipedia / Wikidata** — supplementary descriptions and historical context

The full report, including a state-by-state comparison table, methodology, and FAQ, is available at: **https://soaktrail.com/reports/hot-springs-american-west-2026**

### About Soak Trail

Soak Trail is an independent research project documenting natural hot springs across the American West. The project grew from years of road trips through the region's back roads and mountain passes — stopping at hot springs along the way and noticing how hard it was to find reliable, current information about where to soak.

Founder Chester Beard is an outdoors explorer and conservation photographer based in the Pacific Northwest. He has worked as a photographer for The Nature Conservancy and served on the Washington State Executive Council of the Sierra Club. That background in conservation and field work shaped Soak Trail's approach: combine public data sources (USGS, OpenStreetMap, NOAA) with field verification to produce accurate, accessible guides for natural hot springs.

The project covers 6 regional sites:
- [Washington Hot Springs](https://www.washingtonhotsprings.com)
- [Alaska Hot Springs](https://www.alaskahotsprings.com)
- [Soak Colorado](https://www.soakcolorado.com)
- [Shasta Hot Springs](https://www.shastahotsprings.com)
- [Soak the Rockies](https://www.soaktherockies.com)
- [Desert Soak](https://www.desertsoak.com)
