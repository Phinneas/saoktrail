<p><strong>Short answer:</strong> There are no hot springs in Mount Shasta city itself. The springs in town, like the Headwaters at City Park, are cold. In California, the closest open natural soak is Hunt Hot Springs on Kosk Creek near Big Bend. It is about 92 miles and roughly 2 hours 15 minutes from Mount Shasta city, or about 1.5 hours from Redding. Stewart Mineral Springs is only 27 minutes away, but it no longer offers public soaking.</p>

<p>Nestled in the shadow of majestic Mount Shasta lies a collection of natural geothermal wonders, though the landscape of accessible hot springs has changed significantly in recent years. While the region's mineral-rich waters have drawn visitors for centuries, many once-popular destinations have closed, making the remaining natural springs all the more precious.</p><p>You'll discover that today's Mount Shasta hot springs experience centers primarily around natural, undeveloped pools on private property where respectful access is still permitted. While the era of developed facilities has largely ended, these primitive springs offer an authentic connection to the area's geothermal heritage. Whether you're seeking a peaceful soak after a long hike or hoping to embrace the area's spiritual energy, the remaining accessible springs provide a more rustic but deeply rewarding escape into Northern California's stunning wilderness.</p><h2 id="understanding-mount-shastas-volcanic-hot-springs">Understanding Mount Shasta's Volcanic Hot Springs</h2><p>Mount Shasta is an active stratovolcano and one of the most prominent peaks in the Cascade Range. Underground magma chambers heat groundwater, which then rises through fractures in the volcanic rock, creating the hot springs we can enjoy today. The Hunt and Kosk Creek areas specifically have surface springs because of their proximity to these geothermal conduits running through the Cascade volcanic arc.</p><p>What makes these waters unique is their mineral composition, drawn directly from the volcanic rock they pass through. As the superheated water dissolves minerals from the surrounding basalt and andesite, it emerges enriched with calcium, magnesium, sulfur, and silica—the same minerals that have attracted soakers for generations.</p><h3 id="the-geology-youre-soaking-in">The Geology You're Soaking In</h3><p>When you visit Hunt Hot Springs, you'll notice travertine deposits—calcium carbonate formations created when mineral-rich water cools and deposits its dissolved minerals. These cream-colored formations give the pools their distinctive coloring and create natural barriers that help contain the water.</p><p>Some pools are significantly hotter than others because of their proximity to the primary geothermal source. The upper concrete pools, built closest to where hot water emerges from the ground, can reach 120°F, while lower pools cool naturally as water flows downstream and mixes with creek water.</p><p><strong>Did you know?</strong> Mount Shasta last erupted in 1786, and geologists consider it one of the most likely Cascade volcanoes to erupt again. The geothermal activity that creates these hot springs is directly connected to the active magma system beneath the mountain.</p><h2 id="popular-hot-springs-around-mount-shasta">Popular Hot Springs Around Mount Shasta</h2><h3 id="hunt-hot-springs">Hunt Hot Springs</h3><p>Hunt Hot Springs provides the most accessible natural hot springs experience near Mount Shasta, featuring multiple pools along Kosk Creek about 2 hours 15 minutes from Mount Shasta city (about 1.5 hours from Redding).</p><p><strong>Current Access Information:</strong></p><ul><li>Free access on private property (landowner permits public use)</li><li>Hipcamp reservations now recommended for guaranteed access</li><li>0.5-mile hike downstream from parking area</li><li>Seasonal access dependent on road conditions</li><li>Pack in, pack out required—no facilities</li></ul><h4 id="individual-pool-experiences">Individual Pool Experiences</h4><p><strong>Upper Concrete Pools (Twin Connected Tubs)</strong></p><ul><li><strong>Temperatures:</strong> 117°F and 106°F</li><li><strong>Capacity:</strong> 4-6 people total</li><li><strong>Best for:</strong> Those who love very hot soaks</li><li><strong>Features:</strong> Rock and concrete construction, furthest from creek, most privacy</li></ul><p>These were built by previous visitors using concrete and natural rock. The intense heat makes them ideal for therapeutic soaking but requires caution—limit time to 15-20 minutes before cooling off.</p><p><strong>Natural Riverside Pool (Small)</strong></p><ul><li><strong>Temperature:</strong> ~103°F</li><li><strong>Capacity:</strong> 2 people comfortably</li><li><strong>Best for:</strong> Couples, intimate soaking experiences</li><li><strong>Features:</strong> All-natural rock walls, beautiful creek views, Mother Nature's perfect design</li></ul><p>This pool offers the most scenic setting, positioned right alongside Kosk Creek with the sound of flowing water enhancing your soak.</p><p><strong>Lower Riverside Pool (Larger)</strong></p><ul><li><strong>Base Temperature:</strong> ~100°F (adjustable)</li><li><strong>Capacity:</strong> 4-5 people</li><li><strong>Best for:</strong> Groups, temperature customization</li><li><strong>Features:</strong> Natural rock construction, can control temperature by allowing creek flow</li></ul><p>The unique feature here is temperature control—by strategically moving rocks, you can allow cold creek water to mix in, creating your perfect soaking temperature.</p><p><strong>Kosk Creek Cooling Stations</strong> Multiple access points along the creek allow for contrast therapy—alternating between hot pools and cold creek plunges. This practice, popular in Scandinavian countries, increases circulation and creates an invigorating experience.</p><h4 id="unique-features">Unique Features</h4><p>A fascinating aspect of Hunt Hot Springs is the numerous spots where hot spring water percolates up throughout the area. Nearby hillsides, the creek bank, and other locations all have water bubbling to the surface. Perhaps future volunteers will develop additional pools at these seeps—who's up for the challenge?</p><h4 id="directions-to-hunt-hot-springs">Directions to Hunt Hot Springs</h4><p>From Mount Shasta city (about 92 miles, roughly 2 hours 15 minutes):</p><ol><li>Take CA-89 south about 56 miles toward Burney, then CA-299 west about 20 miles to Big Bend Road (from Redding, take CA-299 east about 35 miles instead)</li><li>Turn onto Big Bend Road in the town of Big Bend</li><li>Cross the river bridge—after crossing, Big Bend Road becomes Summit Lake Road</li><li>Continue on Summit Lake Road for approximately 2 miles</li><li>Stay left at the fork (roughly 1 mile after the road name change)</li><li>Look for the Kosk Creek bridge—park before the bridge, do not cross it</li><li>Hike 0.5 miles downstream on the east side of Kosk Creek</li></ol><p><strong>GPS Coordinates for Parking:</strong> Search "Big Bend Road and Kosk Creek" to find the parking area near the bridge.</p><p><strong>Important:</strong> This is private property. The property now operates as Pneuma Retreat Center, offering lodging and retreat experiences but no public hot springs access.</p><p>Historically established in 1875 as California's oldest mineral springs, Stewart featured private cedar bathing rooms, saunas, and mineral baths that drew visitors for over 140 years. The property maintains its spiritual retreat focus but no longer provides the hot springs amenities it was famous for.</p><h3 id="nearby-alternative-options">Nearby Alternative Options</h3><p><strong>Wilbur Hot Springs</strong> (about 3.5 to 4 hours south)</p><ul><li>Natural medicinal mineral springs resort</li><li>Three temperature-controlled pools (98°F, 104°F, 110°F)</li><li>Day-use and overnight accommodation options</li><li>Clothing-optional natural setting</li><li>Full facilities and amenities</li></ul><p><strong>Sierra Hot Springs</strong> (about 4.5 hours southeast near Sierraville)</p><ul><li>Community-run nonprofit hot springs</li><li>Multiple pools and sauna facilities</li><li>Alpine valley setting on 700 acres</li><li>Year-round access with seasonal variations</li><li>Membership or day-use options available</li></ul>

The drive itself becomes part of your hot springs adventure, showcasing the volcanic landscape that creates these geothermal features.</p><h2 id="best-times-to-visit-mount-shasta-hot-springs">Best Times to Visit Mount Shasta Hot Springs</h2><p>Mount Shasta's remaining accessible hot springs offer unique experiences throughout the year, though seasonal conditions significantly impact access to these primarily natural, undeveloped locations. The optimal timing for your visit depends on weather, road conditions, private property access, and your comfort with primitive facilities.</p><h3 id="summer-june-august">Summer (June-August)</h3><p>Summer provides the most reliable access to Hunt Hot Springs and other natural pools, with average temperatures between 75-85°F and minimal precipitation. Road conditions remain generally good through October, but shorter daylight hours require earlier starts for longer hikes to remote springs. This is the sweet spot for many hot springs enthusiasts.</p><p><strong>Best pools:</strong> The upper concrete pools (117°F and 106°F) retain heat better in cooler air and feel especially therapeutic when air temperatures drop.</p><p><strong>Fall colors:</strong> Peak foliage occurs late September through early October, with golden aspens and red maples surrounding the springs.</p><p><strong>Photography:</strong> Golden hour lighting from 5-6pm creates spectacular conditions for mountain photography while soaking.</p><p><strong>Fewer crowds:</strong> Mid-week visits in fall often mean having the springs entirely to yourself.</p><p><strong>Bonus:</strong> Nearby apple picking season coincides with prime soaking weather—pick fresh apples, then enjoy them during your evening soak.</p><h3 id="winter-december-february">Winter (December-February)</h3><p>Winter severely limits access to most springs due to snow and impassable roads. Hunt Hot Springs may be completely inaccessible during heavy snow periods. The few springs that remain reachable offer a magical winter experience but require 4WD vehicles with chains and comprehensive winter gear.</p><p><strong>Access reality check:</strong> Most visitors cannot access Hunt Hot Springs December through February. Always call the ranger station before attempting winter visits.</p><p><strong>If accessible:</strong> The upper pools (117°F and 106°F) work best—the extreme heat contrasts beautifully with frozen surroundings.</p><p><strong>Required equipment:</strong> 4WD vehicle with chains, emergency winter kit, warm changing clothes, hand warmers, waterproof boots.</p><p><strong>Contrast therapy:</strong> The experience of alternating between 117°F water and snow creates an unforgettable thermal experience. The steam rising into freezing air is otherworldly.</p><p><strong>Safety:</strong> Never attempt winter access alone. Road conditions can change rapidly, and getting stranded in this remote area poses serious risks.</p><h3 id="spring-march-may">Spring (March-May)</h3><p>Spring features unpredictable conditions with potential road washouts from snowmelt and rain. While wildflowers bloom around accessible springs, verify road conditions before attempting visits to avoid getting stranded. April and May bring some of the year's most beautiful scenery but also the most challenging access.</p><p><strong>Wildflower timing:</strong> Late April through May brings peak wildflower blooms in meadows surrounding the springs—lupines, Indian paintbrush, and balsamroot create stunning displays.</p><p><strong>Creek danger:</strong> Spring snowmelt creates high water conditions in Kosk Creek, making crossings dangerous or impossible. Some pools may be flooded.</p><p><strong>Road washouts:</strong> Always call the Mount Shasta Ranger Station (530-926-4511) before spring trips. Recent storms may have damaged roads.</p><p><strong>Best pools:</strong> Pool accessibility varies as water levels change. <h2 id="hot-springs-etiquette-what-to-expect">Hot Springs Etiquette &amp; What to Expect</h2><h3 id="clothing-optional-guidelines">Clothing Optional Guidelines</h3><p><strong>The Reality:</strong> Hunt Hot Springs operates as clothing optional in common practice. With no official policy governing this private property, both nude and clothed bathers share the pools peacefully. First-time visitors should expect to encounter nude soakers, especially during off-peak hours when privacy is greater.</p><p><strong>Etiquette:</strong></p><ul><li>Respect others' choices whether they choose clothing or not</li><li>No staring or photography of others without explicit permission</li><li>Maintain respectful distance and conversation volume</li><li>If uncomfortable with nudity, visit early mornings when fewer people are present</li><li>Many regular visitors prefer the freedom of clothing-optional soaking; newcomers adapt quickly to the accepting atmosphere</li></ul><h3 id="primitive-hot-springs-behavior">Primitive Hot Springs Behavior</h3><p><strong>Do:</strong></p><ul><li>Pack out everything you pack in, including toilet paper and food waste</li><li>Arrive with a clean body—shower beforehand if possible to keep pools pristine</li><li>Keep voices low and respectful of the natural soundscape</li><li>Limit group size to 6 or fewer people</li><li>Share pools graciously when others arrive</li><li>Rinse visible dirt from feet and body before entering pools</li><li>Use designated areas for changing if present</li></ul><p><strong>Don't:</strong></p><ul><li>Use soap, shampoo, conditioner, or any products in or near pools</li><li>Bring glass containers (broken glass is a serious hazard)</li><li>Consume alcohol excessively or become rowdy</li><li>Play loud music—nature provides the soundtrack</li><li>Leave any trash whatsoever, even "biodegradable" items like orange peels</li><li>Disturb natural rock formations or pool structures</li><li>Set up camp directly at the springs (camping is not permitted here)</li></ul><h3 id="privacy-photography">Privacy &amp; Photography</h3><p>Ask permission before photographing anyone, even if they're in the background of your shot. <h2 id="accessibility-mobility-information">Accessibility &amp; Mobility Information</h2><h3 id="physical-accessibility-assessment">Physical Accessibility Assessment</h3><p><strong>Terrain Reality Check:</strong> The 0.5-mile hike to Hunt Hot Springs follows an uneven, primitive trail with no improvements for accessibility. Creek crossings may be necessary depending on the season and water levels. Rocky, potentially slippery surfaces surround all pools. <h3 id="who-should-visit-hunt-hot-springs">Who Should Visit Hunt Hot Springs?</h3><p><strong>Best suited for:</strong></p><ul><li>Budget-conscious travelers seeking free experiences</li><li>Wilderness and outdoor enthusiasts</li><li>Those seeking authentic, undeveloped natural settings</li><li>Photographers looking for scenic hot springs</li><li>People who value solitude and quiet</li></ul><p><strong>Better alternatives exist for:</strong></p><ul><li>Families with young children → Wilbur Hot Springs (safer, supervised)</li><li>Those wanting amenities and comfort → Sierra or Wilbur (full facilities)</li><li>Limited mobility visitors → Developed resorts with accessibility features</li><li>First-time hot springers → Sierra Hot Springs (guided experience, orientation)</li><li>Anyone uncomfortable with primitive conditions → Commercial hot springs resorts</li></ul><h2 id="frequently-asked-questions">Frequently Asked Questions</h2><p><strong>Are there any hot springs near Mount Shasta?</strong></p><p>Yes, but not in town. The springs in Mount Shasta city are cold. The closest open natural soak in California is Hunt Hot Springs on Kosk Creek near Big Bend, about 2 hours 15 minutes by road. Hunt has several pools from about 100 to 120°F. Kosk Hot Springs is in the same area and is more primitive. See the table at the top of this page for drive times and status.</p><p><strong>Is Stewart Mineral Springs still open?</strong></p><p>No. Stewart Mineral Springs is permanently closed to public soaking. The bathhouse first shut during the 2020 COVID-19 closures. On May 1, 2020, the owners announced it would not reopen (<a href="https://www.stewartsprings.org/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">StewartSprings.org</a>). The property now runs as Pneuma Retreat Center, with lodging and group retreats. The bathhouse, sauna and massage rooms remain closed. Read <a href="/blog/stewart-mineral-springs">our Stewart Mineral Springs page</a> for the full history and the closest places to soak instead.</p><p><strong>Are there any free hot springs in California?</strong></p><p>Yes. Near Mount Shasta, Hunt Hot Springs and Kosk Hot Springs are both free. They sit on private land where the owner allows public use. Across California, many undeveloped springs on public land are free to soak, like <a href="/blog/travertine-hot-springs">Travertine Hot Springs</a> near Bridgeport. Some free springs still charge for parking or trail access, so check the guide for each one before you go.</p><p><strong>Who should not use hot springs?</strong></p><p>Skip hot springs if you are pregnant, have been drinking, or have low blood pressure. If you have a heart condition or take medication for high blood pressure, talk to your doctor first. Hot water lowers blood pressure and strains the heart. Children under 5 should stay out of hot water, and people with seizure disorders, open cuts, fresh tattoos or recent diarrhea should wait (<a href="https://health.clevelandclinic.org/are-hot-tubs-good-or-bad-for-you" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Cleveland Clinic</a>). If you are pregnant and still choose to soak, HealthLink BC advises water below 102°F and no more than 10 minutes at a time (<a href="https://www.healthlinkbc.ca/healthwise/pregnancy-hot-tub-and-sauna-use" target="_blank" rel="noopener">HealthLink BC</a>). Everyone should keep their head above water. The CDC links rare brain infections to people putting their heads under water in hot springs (<a href="https://www.cdc.gov/naegleria/media/pdfs/naegleria_factsheet508c.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener">CDC</a>).</p><p><strong>How often should you soak in hot springs?</strong></p><p>There is no medical rule for how many days a week to soak. Time per soak matters more. Keep each dip to 15 minutes or less, then get out and cool off before going back in (<a href="https://health.clevelandclinic.org/are-hot-tubs-good-or-bad-for-you" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Cleveland Clinic</a>). At Hunt, the upper pools run 106 to 117°F, so shorter dips are smarter there. Drink water between soaks. Get out right away if you feel dizzy, faint or your heart races.</p><p><strong>Do I need reservations for Hunt Hot Springs?</strong></p><p>Hipcamp now offers reservation options for the Big Bend area, which can guarantee access and support the local landowner. While not always strictly required, reservations are strongly recommended for weekend visits and peak season (June-September) to avoid conflicts and ensure the landowner's continued permission for public access.</p><p><strong>Is Hunt Hot Springs clothing optional?</strong></p><p>Yes, clothing optional is common practice and widely accepted. Both nude and clothed bathers share the pools respectfully. Expect to encounter nude soakers, especially during off-peak hours and weekdays. The welcoming atmosphere accommodates all comfort levels.</p><p><strong>What's the best pool for beginners?</strong></p><p>The lower riverside pool works best for first-time visitors. At approximately 100°F base temperature (adjustable with creek water), it offers the most comfortable temperature and easiest access from the main trail. You can customize the temperature by allowing cold creek water to mix in, making it perfect while you acclimate to hot springs soaking.</p><p><strong>Can I camp at Hunt Hot Springs?</strong></p><p>No camping is permitted directly at the springs themselves. The closest established camping is McArthur-Burney Falls Memorial State Park (about 40 road miles away) or dispersed Forest Service camping in the surrounding Shasta-Trinity National Forest. Some Hipcamp private properties near Big Bend offer camping with hot springs access.</p><p><strong>Is it safe to visit alone?</strong></p><p>Many people visit solo without any issues. Weekday mornings (6am-10am) tend to be safest and most peaceful for solo travelers. Weekend visits bring more people, offering both safety in numbers and less solitude. Always trust your instincts about who's present, tell someone your plans, and consider bringing a friend for your first visit if you're unfamiliar with the area.</p><p><strong>How long does the hike take?</strong></p><p>The 0.5-mile hike takes 15-20 minutes each way on relatively easy but uneven terrain. Factor in 30-40 minutes total for round-trip hiking, plus whatever time you'd like to soak. Most visitors spend 1-3 hours at the springs depending on pool availability and weather.</p><p><strong>What should I bring?</strong></p><p><strong>Essentials:</strong> Water (1 liter minimum per person), towel, footwear that can get wet, small dry bag for valuables and phone.</p><p><strong>Recommended:</strong> Water shoes for creek crossings, headlamp if staying past dusk, light snacks, biodegradable sunscreen, hat for sun protection, extra layers for after your soak.</p><p><strong>Can I bring my dog?</strong></p><p>Dogs are generally allowed but must be leashed and well-behaved at all times. Not all visitors appreciate dogs in or near pools, and some people come specifically for a quiet, pet-free experience. Consider leaving dogs at camp for the most respectful experience. Never allow dogs in the soaking pools.</p><p><strong>What's the water temperature range?</strong></p><p>Pool temperatures range from approximately 100°F (lower adjustable pool) to 120°F (hottest upper concrete pool). The variety lets you choose your preferred temperature or alternate between pools. Kosk Creek provides cold water (typically 45-55°F) for cooling off between hot soaks—this contrast therapy is invigorating and therapeutic.</p><p><strong>When is the least crowded time?</strong></p><p>Weekday mornings between 6am-10am see the fewest visitors and offer the most solitude. Fall (September-November) and spring (April-May) seasons are less crowded than summer overall. Summer weekends and holidays, especially in July and August, bring the most visitors. Consider visiting during the shoulder seasons for the best balance of good weather and low crowds.</p><h2 id="-1"></h2><p>Mount Shasta's accessible hot springs offer a unique blend of natural healing and scenic beauty, though today's experience is quite different from the developed facilities of the past. With most commercial operations now closed, your hot springs adventure will center around Hunt Hot Springs and other natural pools that require more planning and respect for private property access.</p><p>These remaining mineral-rich waters still provide significant physical and mental wellness benefits while connecting you to the area's rich geological heritage. However, success requires careful preparation, including verifying current access conditions, respecting private property guidelines, and being prepared for primitive facilities where you pack in everything you need and pack out all trash.</p><p>The Mount Shasta hot springs experience has evolved from convenient resort-style soaking to a more adventurous pursuit that rewards those willing to hike, research access conditions, and embrace a leave-no-trace wilderness ethic. For those who make the effort, these geothermal treasures remain among Northern California's most rewarding natural experiences.</p><p>Remember to always verify current access conditions and property permissions before visiting, as situations can change rapidly for springs located on private land. Check with the Mount Shasta Ranger Station at (530) 926-4511 for the latest updates on road conditions, permits, and access status.</p>
