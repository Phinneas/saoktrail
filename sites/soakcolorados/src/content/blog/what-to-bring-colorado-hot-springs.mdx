Colorado hot springs come in every flavor — historic resorts in Glenwood Springs, clothing-optional retreats in the San Juans, and wild pools at the end of a trail. But between the altitude (most springs sit between 5,700 and 11,000+ feet), intense sun, and 40-degree daily temperature swings, showing up underprepared can cut a soak short. Here's everything to pack, whether you're headed to a developed resort or a backcountry pool.

## The Essentials: Any Spring, Any Season

- **Swimsuit (quick-dry)** — required at nearly all developed springs; a spare is smart
- **Towel** — resorts usually rent them for a few dollars, but primitive pools provide nothing; a compact microfiber towel is ideal for hike-in springs
- **Water bottle (32 oz+)** — altitude plus hot mineral water dehydrates you fast; pack more than you think you need
- **Water shoes or grippy sport sandals** — natural pool bottoms are rocky and algae-slick; flip-flops fall off and won't protect your feet in the water
- **Dry bag or plastic bag** — for wet suits and towels in the car
- **Change of clothes and underwear**
- **Mineral/non-nano sunscreen and SPF lip balm** — UV exposure is significantly stronger at 7,000+ feet, even on cloudy days
- **Cash and card** — entry fees typically run $5–$30+, and some rural springs are cash-only
- **Snacks** — see below, and don't skip these

## Food and Drink

Soaking in hot water makes you sweat and burn through salt and calories faster than most people expect. Dizziness is usually dehydration or low blood sugar — both preventable.

- **Salty snacks:** trail mix, jerky, chips, electrolyte drink mix or tablets
- **Substantial snacks:** sandwiches or fruit for longer days
- **Cooler and ice** for summer trips
- **Limit alcohol** — combined with altitude and heat, it's a fast track to lightheadedness. Alternate soaks with water instead.

## Clothing and Layers

Colorado weather changes fast, and the walk from pool to changing room in January is no joke.

**Summer:**
- Light layers, sun hat, sunglasses
- Rain shell — afternoon thunderstorms are common in July and August

**Winter:**
- Insulated coat, beanie, and gloves — wet hair can freeze in minutes
- Waterproof slip-on sandals or boots you can shed easily at the pool edge
- Microspikes if a primitive spring requires an icy trail approach
- A robe or oversized parka to change under

**Year-round:**
- Flip-flops for locker rooms and showers at resorts
- Rinse your swimsuit after your soak — mineral water is hard on elastic

## If You're Hiking Into (or Camping Near) a Spring

Several Colorado springs require real effort to reach — Rainbow Hot Springs is a roughly 5-mile hike into the Weminuche Wilderness, and Conundrum Hot Springs near Aspen requires an overnight permit. A few others allow on-site or nearby camping (Valley View Hot Springs, for example).

**Backpacking checklist:**
- Tent with rainfly and stakes
- Sleeping bag rated to 20–30°F — nights stay cold even in July at 9,000–11,000 feet
- Sleeping pad
- Camp stove, meals, and extra snacks
- Headlamp (use red-light mode near shared pools after dark)
- Water filter — soak in spring water, but never drink it
- Rain shell and warm layers
- Toilet kit: trowel or WAG bag, toilet paper, hand sanitizer
- Bear-resistant food storage where required
- Offline maps or GPS — primitive springs rarely have cell service

For car camping, add camp chairs, a lantern, and a cooler.

## What NOT to Bring

- **Glass of any kind** — banned at essentially every spring, for obvious reasons
- **Alcohol** — prohibited at many springs, including family-friendly and clothing-optional sites; check the rules for your destination
- **Speakers and drones** — soaking is a quiet activity
- **Pets** — most springs don't allow dogs (service animals excepted)
- **Lotions, oils, and freshly applied sunscreen** — they clog filters and pollute natural pools; apply well before arriving or rinse off first
- **Valuables** — leave them at your lodging or use lockers where available

## Resort vs. Primitive Springs: What Changes

| Item | Developed resort | Primitive/wild spring |
|---|---|---|
| Towel | Usually rentable | Bring your own |
| Changing room | Yes | Improvise — towel wrap or robe |
| Drinking water | Fountains or for sale | Pack it all in |
| Trash | Receptacles available | Pack out everything |
| Lighting | Yes | Headlamp essential |
| Food | Café at some | Bring everything |
| Fee | $5–$30+ | Free (or backcountry permit) |

## Quick-Grab Checklist

- Swimsuit (+ spare)
- Towel / microfiber towel
- Water bottle (32 oz+)
- Water shoes or sport sandals
- Snacks + electrolytes
- Sunscreen + SPF lip balm
- Change of clothes
- Bag for wet gear
- Cash/card for entry fees
- **Winter add-ons:** coat, beanie, waterproof sandals, microspikes
- **Backcountry add-ons:** tent, 20–30°F bag, pad, headlamp, water filter, stove, toilet kit

## FAQ

**Do I need to bring my own towel?**
At developed resorts, usually no — most rent them for a few dollars. For primitive springs, always yes.

**Can I wear a t-shirt and shorts in the water?**
Most developed springs require proper swimwear. Check the rules on each spring's page before you go.

**Can I bring a cooler?**
Yes at most outdoor springs, but glass is always prohibited — use cans and reusable containers.

**How much water should I drink?**
Plan on at least a liter per person for a couple hours of soaking, and sip between soaks — not just after.

**Are kids allowed?**
Many springs welcome families during the day; some go adults-only or clothing-optional after dark. Each spring's page lists its policies.
